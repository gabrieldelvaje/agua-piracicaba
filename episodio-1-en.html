Why is Piracicaba running short of water?

The answer does not begin and end in the river. This episode follows the path of water from intake to tap to understand consumption, urban growth, losses, and the infrastructure that sustains the supply system.

Today, Piracicaba uses the Piracicaba and Corumbataí rivers as water sources. According to Semae, the Corumbataí River accounts for more than 80% of the city’s water supply. Official source ↗︎

Before it became the Water Museum, this was where Piracicaba’s water supply began.

In 1887, the first intake and pumping station carried water from the Piracicaba River to the city center. The fountain in Cathedral Square marked the arrival of piped water.

As the city grew, the system changed: Semae was created, Cantareira altered the regional context, and in the 1980s Piracicaba also began drawing water from the Corumbataí River, which today accounts for more than 80% of supply. The old station became the Water Museum in 2000.

1887

Piped water reaches the city center. The first intake and pumping station is inaugurated; water flows from the fountain in Cathedral Square.

1952

The service moves under City Hall. Water supply and sewerage come under the Department of Water and Sewage.

1969

Semae is created. The municipal utility begins organizing and expanding the city’s water and sewer system. Semae history ↗︎

1974

Cantareira changes the regional context. The first authorization allows water to be transferred from the Piracicaba basin to supply the São Paulo Metropolitan Region. Cantareira System · ANA ↗︎

1980s

Piracicaba turns to another water source. As the Piracicaba River’s water quality gradually worsens, the Corumbataí intake and the Capim Fino treatment plant begin operating.

2000

The historic station becomes a museum. The 1887 complex is preserved as the Water Museum. Water Museum ↗︎

2024

The Corumbataí supports most of the system. In 2024, more than 80% of the city’s supply came from this source. Current supply ↗︎

What is being done.

Current interventions target different parts of the system: increasing treated-water production and strengthening distribution to areas that grew beyond the capacity of existing mains.

Capim Fino treatment plant increased from 1,500 to 2,000 L/s of capacity after the expansion delivered in August 2026. ↗︎ The Dois Córregos–Cecap transmission main is under construction in the eastern part of the city. The project includes 4.2 km of 500-mm pipeline along Avenida Pompeia and surrounding streets, with completion expected by August 2027. ↗︎ Santa Teresinha is replacing old mains in a project that calls for replacing 19.7 km of mains and transmission lines plus 2,046 household connections as part of the loss-reduction and district-metering program in the northern region. ↗︎

Piracicaba was an early pioneer in piped water. Today, the challenge is making the network keep pace with the city.

Piracicaba was an early pioneer in water supply. In 2026, 442,635 residents depend on infrastructure that must keep pace with the city’s growth.

Between 1997 and 2021, residential customer units grew 103,5%, while the network grew 49,7%. The bottleneck is not only producing water, but distributing it.

The Municipal Sanitation Plan calls for replacing about 869 km of mains by 2055, as well as expanding capacity and transmission mains. Even so, recent incidents show that network failures still interrupt supply.

Producing more water is not enough. The network is what turns production into supply.

Sources and methodology.

The charts and comparisons in this episode were built from historical series from Piracicaba em Dados/IPPLAP, SNIS data reproduced in the 2026 Municipal Sanitation Plan, Semae documents, and dated news reports. Each indicator keeps the period actually available in its source.

To avoid mixing incompatible time windows, partial 2022 data are excluded from 1997–2021 comparisons; the losses series runs from 2010 to 2022; production by treatment plant uses 2022; and projects and incidents were updated through October 2026. Calculations, processed datasets, validation, and limitations are documented in the repository.

Methodology

How the datasets were converted, calculated, and compared.

PDF conversion

Text tables were extracted while preserving published values. Thousands separators were removed and decimal commas converted to periods. Unavailable values or discontinued categories were kept as empty fields, without imputation.

Partial periods

Rows explicitly identified as partial receive periodo_completo=false. This includes 2022 in several historical series and 2019 in the Piracicaba River flow series.

Residential consumption

Average monthly consumption per customer unit is calculated as annual residential consumption ÷ residential customer units ÷ 12. The comparison ends in 2021 because the historical 2022 values represent January only.

Losses

Loss indicators were not reconstructed. The episode uses the official SNIS series transcribed from Table 25 of the 2026 Municipal Sanitation Plan.

Validation

Source discrepancies are preserved and flagged with quality_flag, without automatic correction of published values.

Validation and limitations

Inconsistencies retained in the project so the original source is not silently rewritten.

Partial series

In 2022, consumption, customer units, network, connections, and production/distribution in the historical PDFs include January only. The 2019 flow series covers January through October, and 2022 precipitation also contains January only.

Production and distribution

In 2010, the source reports total distributed volume greater than production. In 2019, annual totals are not compatible with monthly averages multiplied by 12. In 2022, besides being partial, the monthly average and January total do not match. None of these values was adjusted.

Population

The SEADE dataset was kept exactly as published. Some rows have age-group sums that do not match the total; the difference is calculated in the CSV for auditing.

2023 losses

The 2026 Municipal Sanitation Plan reports 55.40% in one section and 54.50% in another. Both records remain documented; this episode uses 2022 (53.93%), which appears in the SNIS table series.
