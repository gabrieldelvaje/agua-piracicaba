Why did Piracicaba turn to the Corumbataí River for water?

How a second river went from backup source to the city’s main water supply.

Follow the story Collection: IHGP · Flickr ↗︎

The river that runs through Piracicaba is not the same one that comes out of the city’s taps.

The Piracicaba gave the city its name and supplied it for decades. As raw-water quality deteriorated and the city sought greater water security, Piracicaba became increasingly dependent on another source.

In September 2024, Semae reported that about 80% of the city’s water supply came from the Corumbataí and 20% from the Piracicaba. It is a snapshot of that moment, but it shows the scale of the reversal.

Nov. 7, 1978 · O Diário “Authorities conclude: Piracicaba is drinking contaminated water” Water quality had become front-page news. The crisis was no longer only about the river: it had reached the water consumed by the city.

Mar. 3, 2000 · A Tribuna Piracicabana “Piracicaba River will stop supplying the city in April” Two decades later, the shift appears explicitly in the press: the Corumbataí was presented as the route toward concentrating the city's supply away from the Piracicaba.

In 2000, Piracicaba tried to concentrate its water supply on the Corumbataí.

The arrangement lasted only a few months. Operational limits at Capim Fino led the city to bring the Piracicaba River back as a supplementary source.

05 · 2022

In 2022, Capim Fino produced almost two thirds of the treated water.

The 65.38% figure measures production at Treatment Plant III (Capim Fino), not the origin of all the water. Treatment Plants I and II (Luiz de Queiroz) can also receive raw water from the Corumbataí. That is why this figure does not contradict the roughly 80% attributed to the Corumbataí in the city’s supply in September 2024.

08 · The cost of dependence

1999

Turning to another river also created a duty to protect it.

In October 1999, Semae began contributing financially to restoration actions in the Corumbataí basin. The logic is simple: once the source sustains the city, protecting its basin is no longer only environmental policy — it becomes part of supply security.

That does not eliminate the need to restore the Piracicaba. The current system still relies on two sources — precisely because water security also depends on redundancy.

Methodology

Chronology

The 1973, 1979, and 1982 milestones come from the project retrospective and are cross-checked with the Municipal Sanitation Plan to confirm the startup of Treatment Plant III.

Production by treatment plant

The 2022 percentages were recalculated from Table 14. The rows sum to 70,719,256 m³; the document’s running text differs by 1 m³, so the page uses the table.

River shares

The 80% figure refers to the Sep. 17, 2024 notice. It is not converted into a time series or directly compared with Treatment Plant III’s 2022 production share.

Press

O Estado de S. Paulo appears only by headline, date, and page. The 2000 Tribuna item was located through Martirani’s thesis and is treated as an announcement, not operational confirmation.

Validation and limitations

Data from different periods

The Municipal Sanitation Plan published in 2026 brings together diagnoses and tables from different years. Each figure is presented with its own reference date.

Rainfall

516.9 mm and 1,177.2 mm are readings from a Civil Defense rain gauge in the Paulista neighborhood, not basin-wide precipitation averages.

Flow and abstraction

The September 2024 values are operational snapshots. The page does not divide flow by abstraction to infer what fraction of the river was being withdrawn.

2026 capacity

2,000 L/s is the announced post-expansion capacity, not production continuously measured at that level.
