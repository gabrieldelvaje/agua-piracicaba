EP02 Waters of the Piracicaba River

The diversion that changed the Piracicaba River

The river was already polluted. Cantareira reduced part of the water that would otherwise continue through the basin. By the late 1970s, Piracicaba turned that crisis into a public funeral.

Understand the funeral Photo: Will Baldine/Jornal de Piracicaba · via Folha de S.Paulo ↗︎
On October 14, 1978, about three thousand Piracicaba residents went to the banks of the Piracicaba River and declared: “the river is dead”.

The act was organized as a symbolic funeral: black clothes and flags, fishermen wearing masks, and a coffin with “SABESP” written on its side turned the river’s degradation into a public scene of mourning and protest.

Main source: O Estado de S. Paulo, Oct. 15, 1978, p. 54. The protest took place the previous day.

The river did not die in a single day.

The crisis built up in stages: industrialization and urban growth increased pressure on the rivers; pollution became increasingly visible; the 1968–69 drought exposed the system’s fragility; and in the 1970s, Cantareira added a new dispute over water. By 1978, that sequence had already become a public mobilization.

In rivers influenced by Cantareira, flows fall mainly during the dry season. With less water, existing pollution has less water available for dilution. RBRH/SciELO ↗︎

Why did São Paulo need more water?

São Paulo grew rapidly while water availability in the Alto Tietê basin was limited. The Cantareira System was built to bring water from neighboring headwaters and expand supply to the metropolis.

There is no pipe taking water from Rua do Porto. The transfer happens in the headwaters, before part of that water would naturally continue downstream through the Piracicaba basin.

The change in flow was stronger near the reservoirs.

A hydrological study using long-term series identified breaks in the flow regime of rivers affected by Cantareira. On the Jaguari, closer to the reservoirs, the average reduction was much larger; at the Piracicaba River gauge farther downstream, the effect was smaller but still appeared mainly during dry months.

The river was already polluted. The problem was having less water for dilution.

In the 1970s, the Piracicaba was already receiving domestic sewage, industrial effluent, and waste from the sugarcane industry.

The flow reduction did not create those pollutants. But especially during drought, it meant less water available to dilute the same pollutant load discharged into the river — increasing contaminant concentrations.

Less water plus more pollution means less oxygen for fish.

Fish kills do not happen because fish “drink pollution.” During episodes of high organic load, microorganisms decompose that material and also consume dissolved oxygen in the water.

With low flow, the same load becomes more concentrated. Oxygen levels can collapse and the river can enter hypoxia or even anoxia. Without enough oxygen, fish suffocate.

Memory of the 1970s
“There were times of the year when dissolved oxygen actually reached zero.”

Luiz Roberto Moretti’s testimony, collected in ESALQ/USP research, is accompanied by accounts of mornings when the river smelled foul and was covered with dead fish. The same research records that urban sewage treatment at the time was on the order of only 6%.

The report portrays an exhausted city: the river nearly reduced to a trickle in the dry season, dark foul-smelling tap water, and a march that turns the environmental crisis into public protest.

The three articles summarize three dimensions of the crisis: pollutant load, collective mobilization, and the everyday experience of poor-quality water. The original pages of Estado de S. Paulo are not reproduced.

Sources and methodology.

This episode combines historical documents, contemporary reporting, academic studies, and hydrological series to reconstruct how pollution, droughts, and operation of the Cantareira System overlapped in the Piracicaba River crisis.

Flow figures are presented using the time windows and units published by the sources. Testimonies and newspapers help reconstruct the experience of the period but are not treated as measurements. Causal relationships are presented only when supported by the documents consulted.

Methodology

How historical documents, flow data, and qualitative evidence were combined.

Timeline

Dates and events were cross-checked across institutional sources, academic literature, and the press. Archive photographs that do not correspond exactly to the event year are identified as contextual images.

Flows

The changes shown for the Jaguari and Piracicaba reproduce the statistical windows used in Frederice & Brandão’s study. They represent regime shifts identified in the series, not a simple split between “before” and “after” 1974.

Piracicaba basin share

Reference values of 33 m³/s for the system and about 31 m³/s from the Piracicaba basin were converted to thousands of liters per second. The approximate 94% share is 31 ÷ 33.

Dilution

The beaker illustration is conceptual: it keeps the same particle load while varying water volume to show how lower flow can increase concentration. It does not represent an actual river sample.

Historical reading

Reports and testimonies are used to document public perception, language, and everyday experience. They complement but do not replace hydrological measurements or environmental reports.

Validation and limitations

What the data support — and what the episode avoids concluding on its own.

Multicausal crisis

The degradation of the Piracicaba River is not attributed exclusively to Cantareira. The episode considers urban, industrial, and agro-industrial pollution together with still-limited sewage treatment at the time, droughts, and reduced water availability.

33 and 31 m³/s

These are reference values associated with the system and the origin of its waters, not real-time measurements or a guarantee that this flow is being transferred on any specific day.

Hydrological breaks

Statistical change points vary among stream gauges. Therefore, the reduction percentages should not be interpreted as the isolated effect of a single year or event.

Historical press

Descriptions such as dark water, foul smell, and dead fish record the experience of the time. When used to explain environmental mechanisms, they are checked against studies and technical sources.
